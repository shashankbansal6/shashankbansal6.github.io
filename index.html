    02Signals

    Microvolts, thousands of times a second

    Each electrode carries field potentials, high-gamma bursts and spikes, buried in noise at tens of microvolts. Every channel has to be amplified, filtered and digitized right next to the tissue, in parallel.

    03The chip I build

    Up to a thousand channels, a microwatt each

    My neural interface system-on-chip puts a low-noise front-end and digitizer behind every electrode, with impedance monitoring built in. I'm integrating it with transparent Neuro-clear probes for simultaneous electrophysiology and optical imaging. Hover the die to explore it.

    04Through the skin

    Power in, data out, no wires

    The next step is to cut the cable. A pair of coils couples the implant to a wearable across the scalp: the wearable's field powers the chip, and the chip backscatters neural data on the same link. No battery and no wires through the skin.

    05Decode, act, feed back

    Closing the loop

    Hundreds of channels stream to the chip in parallel and are decoded into intent. Here, a person with paralysis moves a cursor across their communication board and selects what they need, and every selection sends a stimulation pulse back to the cortex as feedback. Try being the decoder ↓

    06Your turn

    Be the decoder

    Closing the loop means a real person doing a real task. Here, someone with paralysis wants to ask for something on their communication board. Three neurons on one electrode carry their intent: one fires for left, one for right, one for select. Sort each spike while it is in the sort window with ← → ↑ (or tap the buttons), and leave artifacts alone. Mislabel a spike and the cursor goes the wrong way.

      About

      Engineering the interface between brains and machines

      I'm a first-generation PhD candidate in Bioengineering at UC San Diego, advised by Prof. Gert Cauwenberghs in the Integrated Systems Neuroengineering Lab. I work on dynamic interfaces between living and artificial nervous systems.

      The brain is one of the most complex systems we know of, and I'm trying to science and engineer the heck out of it. My focus is the hardware layer: the sensors, the low-power mixed-signal chips that record from them, and the in-memory and neuromorphic circuits that make sense of the data.

      Outside the lab I hike, ski, play squash and take photos.
